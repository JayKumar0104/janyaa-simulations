Simplified: real bulk capacitors, bleed resistors, and safety standards vary by supply — 400 V, the capacitor size used here, and the 50 V threshold are realistic round numbers for teaching, not a spec for any specific power supply. The discharge clock here runs 15× real time so the lesson fits a workshop; real power-supply capacitors can take minutes to tens of minutes to bleed down, which is exactly why the label says "no user-serviceable parts inside." It does eventually leak down on its own — just not on a timescale you would ever wait out. Never open a power supply, even unplugged — this sim is why.

For the curious — the actual numbers

The part being drawn is the bulk capacitor on the high-voltage side of a switched-mode power supply. It charges to roughly the rectified mains voltage and stores energy in the electric field between its plates. This sim models it as:

  • Supply voltage Vs = 400 V
  • Capacitance C = 470 µF = 470 × 10⁻⁶ F
  • Stored energy E = ½CV² → at 400 V that is 37.6 joules, comparable to a 1 kg mass dropped from about 3.8 m
  • Safety threshold: 50 V DC, a widely used rounded teaching figure for the boundary of hazardous DC voltage

Discharge through a resistance follows exponential decay, V(t) = V₀ e^(−t/τ), with time constant τ = RC:

PathResistanceτ = RCHalf-life (τ ln 2)
Leakage only (bleed off)10 MΩ4700 s3258 s ≈ 54 min
Bleed resistor on100 kΩ47 s32.6 s

The "cuts in half every so often" language on the main screen is exactly the half-life t½ = τ ln 2. The sim clock runs at 15 simulated seconds per real second, so with the bleed resistor in circuit one halving takes 32.6 / 15 = 2.17 real seconds. Going 400 → 200 → 100 → 50 V is three halvings, so the safe threshold arrives at about 6.5 real seconds — which agrees with solving the exponential directly: t = τ ln(400/50) = 97.7 s simulated, or 6.52 s real.

With no bleed resistor, one halving takes 3258 / 15 = 217 real seconds, and reaching 50 V takes 4700 ln(8) = 9773 s simulated — 651.6 real seconds, about 10.9 minutes. That is why the meter appears frozen during a short demo.

Note that energy falls with the square of voltage, so it drops far faster than the voltage reading does — by 31 V the stored energy is already down to about 0.6% of its peak. Switching the bleed resistor in mid-discharge does not restart the clock; it changes τ from that instant onward, which is why the curve visibly kinks rather than jumping.
