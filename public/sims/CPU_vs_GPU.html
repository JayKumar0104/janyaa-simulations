Why this is the whole story. A GPU didn't get 32× more math units by being better engineered — it got them by refusing to buy 128 separate control units and 128 separate caches. That purchase decision is permanent and it's why a GPU is spectacular at one kind of work and poor at the other. Training a neural network is millions of copies of the same tiny formula, so every lane stays busy. Simplified: real GPUs group 32 lanes per control unit rather than 16, and the area percentages here are rounded teaching figures.

For the curious — the real terms

What this sim calls "one control unit issuing to a row of lanes" is what the industry calls SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) — one instruction, applied to many pieces of data at once. Real GPUs group 32 lanes to a control unit (a group Nvidia calls a "warp," AMD calls a "wavefront") rather than the 16 drawn here. The "everyone does something different" failure mode is called warp divergence — when threads in the same group need to take different branches of an if-statement, the hardware runs each branch separately and masks off the lanes that don't apply, which is exactly the 1-of-16-lit effect you saw.

A rough real area breakdown: a CPU core often spends only 10–20% of its die area on the actual arithmetic units (ALUs), with the rest split between cache, branch prediction, and out-of-order execution logic built to keep one thread fast. A GPU streaming multiprocessor spends the large majority of its area on ALUs precisely because it isn't paying for any of that per-thread machinery.

Where the "vector calculus" people mention actually shows up: training a model means repeatedly asking "how far is my current guess from correct?" across billions of numbers at once. The tool for measuring distance across many numbers is a straight generalization of the Pythagorean theorem — in 2D, distance = √(a²+b²); across a billion dimensions, it's the exact same idea, just summing a billion squared differences before the square root instead of two. That single repeated operation — square, add, eventually take a root — is itself made of millions of identical small steps, which is exactly the kind of workload the lane-grid on this page is built to run all at once.
