Same wall socket. Same 5 volts. Watch the current gauges, not the voltage.

Simplified: real charging tapers off near full, and many modern fast chargers raise voltage as well as current — 5 V is held fixed on both sides here specifically to isolate the current-limiting effect. Battery energy assumed at 11 watt-hours, a rounded stand-in for a typical phone battery.

For the curious — the real numbers

Electrical power is P = V × I — voltage multiplied by current. Because both ports hold V = 5.0 V, every bit of the difference comes from the current limit:

QuantityWall chargerLaptop port
Voltage5.0 V5.0 V
Current2.4 A0.5 A
Power P = VI12.0 W2.5 W
Charge time t = E/P55.0 min264.0 min
Ratio12.0 / 2.5 = 4.80×

The 0.5 A figure is the standard USB 2.0 port current limit (500 mA); 2.4 A is typical of a high-current USB wall adapter. Battery energy is taken as E = 11.0 Wh, a rounded stand-in for a ~3000 mAh cell at a nominal 3.7 V (3.0 Ah × 3.7 V = 11.1 Wh).

How the animation is calibrated: the wall bar is scaled to finish at exactly t = 7.0 s, so percentPerSecond_wall = 100/7 = 14.286. The laptop bar is not tuned independently — it is derived from the same wattage ratio, percentPerSecond_laptop = (100/7) / 4.8 = 2.976. At 7 s that puts the laptop at 20.83%, displayed rounded to 21%. Change the wattages and the on-screen relationship follows automatically.

What's simplified: real lithium-ion charging uses a constant-current / constant-voltage (CC-CV) profile — current is held near maximum until roughly 70–80% charge, then tapers sharply, so the last stretch takes disproportionately long. Modern fast-charge standards (USB Power Delivery, Quick Charge) also negotiate higher voltages — 9 V, 12 V, 20 V — rather than only raising current, because P = VI grows either way and higher voltage means less current lost as heat in the cable. Holding 5 V on both sides here is a deliberate teaching choice, isolating one variable.
