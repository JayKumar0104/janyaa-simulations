One is a record player.
One has no moving parts.

A hard drive reads your files with a needle floating over a spinning disc. A solid state drive holds them as trapped electricity in a grid of tiny switches. Drop them both and only one has something that can crash into something else.

First: how close is that needle?

The read head floats about 5 nanometers above the disc — roughly 20 atoms. Shrink a jumbo jet to that same proportion and here is what it looks like:

That is a jet doing 900 km/h with one millimetre of air under it — for years, without ever touching down. Now bump the table.

Pick a bump. Watch which drive cares.

What actually happens. The needle rides on a cushion of air made by the spinning disc. Shove it hard enough and the cushion collapses, the needle touches down at 100 km/h of surface speed, and it ploughs a groove through the magnetic coating — the files in that groove are physically gone, not just forgotten. The SSD has no cushion, no needle and no disc; shaking a grid of trapped electrons does nothing to it. Simplified: real flying heights are 3–10 nm and running drives are usually rated near 70 G, so the thresholds here are round teaching numbers.
