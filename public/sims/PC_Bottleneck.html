Why this idea is worth more than the demo. Once you can see the slow station, you stop guessing about upgrades: you find the part that is always busy while the others wait, and you fix that one. The same reasoning works on a checkout queue, a group project, and a kitchen. It also explains why a top-end graphics card in an old machine disappoints people so reliably. Simplified: real frame rendering overlaps several stages at once and the two parts talk back and forth, so a real bottleneck is rarely this clean — but the rule that the slowest stage sets the pace is exactly right.

For the curious — the real idea behind this

This is a case of a general rule in computer science called Amdahl's Law: if a task is split into stages, the overall speed can never beat the speed of the slowest stage — you can make every other stage instant and the total time barely changes. Formally, if a fraction p of a job can be sped up by a factor of s, the best possible overall speedup is 1 / ((1-p) + p/s). It's the same reason a relay race's time is set by its slowest runner, not the average of all four.

Real GPU pipelines don't wait for one frame to fully finish before starting the next — they overlap several frames "in flight" at once (called pipelining), which is why real bottlenecks are messier than this clean two-station model, but the core rule doesn't change.
