Touch a metal table leg and a wooden one in the same room. The metal feels freezing and the wood feels fine. They have been sitting in the same air all night, so they are the same temperature. Your hand is not a thermometer — it can only feel how fast heat is leaving it.

Both blocks are at room temperature. Press touch and watch what happens to your skin, not to the blocks.

The rule underneath. When two things touch, the temperature right at the contact point settles somewhere between them — but not halfway. It lands closer to whichever material can move heat in and out faster. Metal moves heat far faster than skin does, so the contact point drops almost all the way to the metal's temperature and your nerves report "cold." Wood can barely move heat at all, so the contact point stays close to your own 33 °C and feels like nothing. The test that proves it: slide the temperature above your body heat. If metal were simply "colder," a hot metal block would still feel mild. It does the opposite — it burns, for exactly the same reason. Simplified: uses typical values for how fast each material takes up heat, and ignores sweat, pressure and blood flow.

For the curious — the actual property

The material property that decides this is called thermal effusivity, not thermal conductivity alone — it also depends on density and heat capacity. Higher effusivity means a material can absorb or release heat at a surface faster. The contact temperature is a weighted average, weighted by each side's effusivity relative to the other's: T_contact = (e_skin·T_skin + e_material·T_material) / (e_skin + e_material). Rough relative effusivity values used in this sim (skin = 1000): wood 400, stone 2200, steel 8000, aluminium 24000, foam 40 — which is why aluminium (a drink can) feels even colder than steel at the same temperature.
