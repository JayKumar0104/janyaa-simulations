Two cables can look identical on the outside and fit the same socket. What you can't see is how much copper is actually inside. Make the wire thinner or the cable longer and watch how much of the charger's 5 volts never reaches the phone at all — it turns into heat along the way.

Simplified: models the cable as a plain resistor carrying a fixed current and ignores the plug contacts and the charger's own regulation response. The length is counted twice inside the maths — current flows out along one wire and back along another, and both halves add resistance. The 4.5 V pass line is a rounded teaching threshold, not a spec from any real fast-charge standard.

For the curious — the actual formulas

A conductor's resistance is set by what it's made of, how long it is, and how fat it is:

R = ρ L / A

where ρ (rho) is resistivity, a property of the material. For copper ρ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Here L is the round-trip conductor length — current leaves on one conductor and returns on the other, so a 0.75 m cable contains 1.5 m of conductor in the circuit. The cross-sectional area of a round wire of diameter d is A = π(d/2)².

From there:

  • Voltage dropped in the cable: ΔV = I R
  • Voltage delivered: V = Vₛₒₕₖₓₔ − ΔV, with Vₛₒₕₖₓₔ = 5.0 V
  • Power turned into heat: P = I²R

The key scaling: area goes with the square of diameter, so R ∝ 1/d². Doubling the wire diameter from 0.30 mm to 0.60 mm cuts resistance to a quarter, and heat with it (0.357 / 1.426 = 0.25 exactly). That is why a slightly fatter conductor buys so much — and why cheap cables save money precisely there, where you cannot see it.

At the default 0.75 m and 2 A, delivered voltage crosses the 4.5 V pass line at d ≈ 0.358 mm. The heat-glow on the cable is opacity = clamp(P/1.5, 0, 1), and the drawn stroke width is 4 + 20d pixels, so the picture is a direct function of the slider, not an animation.

What's simplified: real cables also lose voltage at the connectors, real chargers push back by raising output voltage when they sense droop, and USB Power Delivery negotiates higher voltages so that the same power flows at lower current — which cuts I²R heating dramatically, since it scales with the square of current.
