A computer building a game frame is an assembly line: the processor sets the scene up, the graphics card draws it. A line only moves as fast as its slowest station — so upgrading the fast one buys you exactly nothing, no matter what it cost.

You have money for one upgrade. Both cost the same. Pick one and watch the big number at the top — not the part you upgraded.

Right now the CPU can only prepare 45 frames a second, and the GPU can draw 120. Guess which upgrade helps before you click.

Why this idea is worth more than the demo. Once you can see the slow station, you stop guessing about upgrades: you find the part that is always busy while the others wait, and you fix that one. The same reasoning works on a checkout queue, a group project, and a kitchen. It also explains why a top-end graphics card in an old machine disappoints people so reliably. Simplified: real frame rendering overlaps several stages at once and the two parts talk back and forth, so a real bottleneck is rarely this clean — but the rule that the slowest stage sets the pace is exactly right.
