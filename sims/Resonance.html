For the curious — resonance, and what it does in the real world

Every object that can wobble has a natural frequency: the rate it swings, rings or vibrates at when you disturb it once and let go. Drive it with a repeating push at that same rate and each push arrives while the object is already moving the right way, so energy keeps piling on instead of cancelling out. That build-up is called resonance.

For a driven, lightly damped oscillator, the steady-state amplitude at a driving rate r (driving rate divided by natural rate) is

amplitude(r) ∝ 1 / sqrt( (1 − r²)² + (2ζr)² )

with the damping ratio ζ = 0.1 used here — light damping, like a real swing that keeps going for a while after you stop pushing. The peak is sharp, not gradual:

Why the timing matters, not the force. The swing always ends up moving at whatever rate you push it, but the phase — where it is in its arc when your push lands — depends on r. Push far too slowly and your hand arrives when the swing is at the top of its arc, momentarily stopped, so you do almost no useful work. Push far too fast and the swing is coming back at you, so you cancel out what it already had. Exactly on rhythm, the push lands while the swing is at the bottom moving away from you at full speed, which is where a push adds the most energy. That is the quarter-turn phase shift you can watch in the animation.

Where this shows up. An opera singer can shatter a wine glass by holding a note at the glass's own natural frequency, loudly enough and long enough for the vibration to build past what the glass can survive. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse of 1940 is the famous engineering example — steady wind set the deck oscillating and the motion grew until the bridge tore itself apart. Engineers today describe that case more precisely as aeroelastic flutter: the bridge's own motion kept re-timing the force the wind applied to it, which is a self-feeding version of the same build-up rather than a fixed outside rhythm happening to match. Soldiers also break step when crossing a footbridge for exactly this reason, and a washing machine walks across the floor when the drum's spin rate passes through the frame's natural frequency on its way up to speed.
