How long would it take
to guess your password?

There is nothing clever about the attack below. It simply tries every possible password, one after another, until one works. What decides whether that takes a coffee break or longer than the universe has existed is only two things: how many characters you used, and how many different characters you allowed yourself to pick from.

For the curious — the speed of the attacker matters as much as the password

The total number of possibilities is charsetlength — the size of the character set multiplied by itself once for every character. That is the same exponent relationship as folding a sheet of paper: each fold doubles the thickness, and each extra password character multiplies the total by the whole character-set size. It is also why the coloured bar on the main screen grows in equal steps as you drag the length slider, even though the milestones underneath it are accelerating wildly — the bar is measured in the same kind of scale the growth happens in.

Everything above assumes an offline attacker: someone who has stolen the site's password file and can guess on their own hardware as fast as it will go, here taken as one billion guesses per second. A live website that locks an account after a few wrong tries is a completely different problem — the attacker's rate, not just the password's length, sets the real-world time. Two things follow from that table, and they are the practical lessons:

  • Locking an account after a few wrong tries is enormously effective. The same six-letter password that falls in a fraction of a second offline holds for about a year against a site that only allows ten tries a second.
  • Length beats cleverness. Going from 8 to 12 full-keyboard characters multiplies the time by about 78 million. No amount of substituting a 3 for an E does anything remotely comparable — and attackers try those substitutions first anyway.

Well-built sites also never store passwords in a form that can be guessed at a billion a second. They run them through a deliberately slow hash function — bcrypt, scrypt or Argon2 — which can cut even an offline attacker's effective rate by many orders of magnitude compared with the one-billion figure used here.
