Simplified: real processors commonly use 32 or 64 bits, not 4 — a 4-bit register is small enough to click through by hand while keeping the exact same rule. The "jump" described here happens at the same relative point (halfway through the range) no matter how many bits are used.

For the curious — the real rule

The encoding on the right is called two's complement, and it is how essentially every modern computer stores negative whole numbers. For an n-bit register holding raw value r:

ConditionSigned value
r < 2n−1signed = r
r ≥ 2n−1signed = r − 2n

For 4 bits that threshold is 8, which is exactly the 0111 → 1000 step this sim highlights. Formally, overflow in signed addition occurs when two operands of the same sign produce a result of the opposite sign. For the simplest case — repeatedly adding 1 — that can only happen once per cycle, at the maximum positive value rolling over.

This exact misconception is documented in the computing-education research already behind this site's other lessons: Herman, Zilles & Loui found that over 40% of students who had passed a full college hardware course still could not reliably determine whether an addition overflowed, or correctly interpret a two's-complement number.
